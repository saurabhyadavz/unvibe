# Direction: the part a model cannot supply

## Why direction comes first

A model has no point of view; it has an average. Left unsteered it returns the mean of every app
in its training data, which is exactly what every competitor also gets. Tim Gabe's test with one
alarm app built four ways makes the point: a bare prompt scored 5 of 15 on his scorecard; adding
skill files and a basic design system reached 8; cramming in every reference, mood board and
system at once dropped to 4, a "Frankenstein" where components stopped matching and spacing went
haywire because nobody was in charge of a single direction; a person deciding what the app should
feel like and what to cut reached 15. More context does not fix a missing decision. [YS7uOXjmJTA]

The models also raise the floor for everyone at once, so whatever they learn becomes the new
commodity ceiling. The direction is the only input that compounds in your favour. [8EAYZe6y3I8]

## The scorecard

Score any design, including your own, on three dimensions from 1 to 5:

- **Point of view.** Does it stand for something? Could you say in a sentence what it believes
  about its user? A 1 is "clean but nobody decided anything". A 5 is a screen where a stranger
  can tell someone chose this.
- **Consistency.** Does it hold together as one system on the hundredth screen, not just the
  first five? Same situation, same pattern; same radius, same shades, same voice.
- **Execution.** Is there real product craft: a decision that matches the function to the goal
  (the alarm you must shake for 15 seconds because tapping does not wake a body), states handled,
  copy that talks like a person.

Report the three numbers at the top of every audit. They tell the reader where the problem is
before the findings do. [YS7uOXjmJTA]

## Decide what premium means to this buyer

"Make it premium" and "make it pop" leave the designer (or the model) to guess. Before any
screen, write down what premium and trustworthy mean to *this* buyer: calm and warm for a
chronic-illness companion, sharp and fast for a trading tool, "a company that will still be here
in a year" for a neobank. High-trust niches (health, money, law) pay the biggest penalty for a
look that says nobody cared, because the customer wonders where else corners were cut.
[pqWPKgfBDiw, 0GMQRCnOQmo]

## One idea, honoured everywhere

Pick the single feeling or promise and let every screen serve it. The alarm app's idea was
"waking up gently": a sunrise gradient on every screen, copy that talks to you ("don't fight it,
time to get up"), numbers in a considered size hierarchy, a mood check as one soft emoji on one
glowing card, and no snooze button because the app is about waking up when the alarm rings. The
idea is what makes the hundredth screen feel like the first. [YS7uOXjmJTA]

A brand palette is not a look. Undertone's founders knew they wanted "the border of clinical and
minimal, not sterile, with personality" and could not draw it; the designer's job was to turn
that sentence into rules (pistachio and sage on off-whites, a charcoal that never goes fully
black, simple lines) and then a system, before touching a single flow. Settle the visual
language before fixing flows, or the fixes come out feeling like five different products.
[YA3QpYIKqNI]

## Bet against the category's hidden assumption

Every category runs on a belief about the user that nobody says out loud, and the leader's
business is usually built on it. Build the opposite and the leader cannot follow without
risking the product that pays its bills.

- Dating assumed you want to look people up first. Timeleft: "No bios, no swiping, no planning";
  a quiz, a group, a restaurant revealed just before dinner. Bumble had to build a separate app
  to try the opposite. [zH0SVLzLBpU]
- Language apps assumed a quick tap-through game. Speak built lessons around speaking out loud
  and "picks efficacy 100% of the time". [zH0SVLzLBpU]
- Health tracking assumed a ring or band plus a membership. Evidation: software only, on the
  watch you already own. Device makers cannot tell you to skip the device. [zH0SVLzLBpU]

Write down what the big apps in your category assume about the user, circle the one their
revenue depends on, and design the opposite. [zH0SVLzLBpU]

## First-principles patterns from the outliers

Four small teams that built million-dollar apps by refusing their category's design rules:

- **Niche depth.** A productivity app for ADHD users does not look like Notion with another
  font. Tiimo turned text rows into coloured, illustrated time blocks sized by duration, because
  parsing a list of strings costs the attention its users have least of. Mass-market apps cannot
  structurally follow into a niche this deep. [HA4yB_sd62Q]
- **Rebuild primitives around new technology.** MyFitnessPal logs a meal in four to five taps.
  Cal AI made the camera the home screen: point, shoot, done; correction is the exception.
  "Founders who add a feature ship a feature. Founders who reset the primitives ship a
  category-defining product." [HA4yB_sd62Q]
- **Remove shame.** Every macro tracker assumed guilt drives compliance. MacroFactor assumed
  users will miss targets, smoothed the data over three weeks, and never showed a red number.
  Result: cleaner data than any competitor, because nobody games a log that does not judge them.
  [HA4yB_sd62Q]
- **Synchronised ritual.** Ladder pins every member of a team to the same workout on the same
  day, so skipping is visible to 5,000 strangers. Turn solo moments into shared ones where you
  can. [HA4yB_sd62Q]

## Design for one user, with opinions

Raycast built for Mac developers who live on the keyboard and made every decision through one
filter: if it did not increase speed, reduce cognitive load, or fit keyboard-driven work, it did
not ship. Zero clutter, a rigid open-type-act model, power revealed as you go. Strong opinions
turn some people away and create the believers, because "people don't rave about the most
options; they rave about speed, clarity and trust." [eWGNjTLK84c]

## Ask why until the reasoning is real

The best design lesson Tim Gabe got at Spotify was a senior designer who looked at each
prototype in silence and asked "why?" until it became clear the choice had not been thought
through. Good design looks nice; great design solves a real problem. Run the same interrogation
on your own screens: for each element, why this, why here, why this size. [bOm9GDv96Tk]

## Outcomes, not screens

Ask what the quickest line between the user and their outcome is, which steps are mechanical
today, and which the user would happily never do again. Repetitive, rule-based, utility flows
are the ones that disappear; emotional, judgement-heavy or interaction-as-the-value flows are
where the interface still matters and delight pays. Wrap sophisticated technology in interfaces
people already know: Discover Weekly looked like any other playlist, and that is why it worked.
Users do not want to meet your algorithm; they want to feel understood. [zRUoPIwCxfw, Tpg0pxKHrCA]

## When building with AI

- Feed it real screens from real apps, not adjectives. Build a small mood board and a distinct
  direction before the first prompt.
- Give it a designer's rules (skill files from design engineers; Emil Kowalski's, Jakub
  Krehel's and Ibelick's are named).
- Then add one thing only you could have: your own icons, your own type, one idea that belongs
  to your company. "Fine is the floor now, so the care has to come from decisions only you would
  make."
- Before each release, name one decision in it that only your company would have made.
- Watch for the AI tells: Inter for everything, purple-to-blue gradients, glossy abstract art,
  the same six layouts nine in ten generated sites land in. [pqWPKgfBDiw]
- Write the requirements before prompting a builder: app overview, pages, functionality per
  page, data needs, aesthetic, build sequence (UI first, then data). One instruction per prompt
  lands better than five. [CfwNxDEXe6I]

## The website is the product's first preview

Customers judge the site before they open the product, and most link site quality to product
quality. Build the site from the product's own parts (its real cards, its real controls, its
real art) so moving from site to app feels like one experience. [pqWPKgfBDiw]

## The agency process, for when the task is a whole product

Six stages, each one de-risking the next: onboarding (one source of truth for decisions and
files); discovery (one strategy workshop, references to align taste, competitors' apps actually
downloaded and used, research only on the decisions that need it, neither months of research nor
jumping to UI); UX (journeys and wire flows for the whole app, deepest on the key flows,
low-fidelity first for buy-in, emotional moments pinned to specific screens now, prototypes for
important interactions); UI (mood boards and several concept directions, then high fidelity and
reusable patterns, with a QA pass on every screen); emotional integration (the pinned moments
that serve business goals, built lightweight and interactive); delivery (edge cases, remaining
states, accessibility, a second QA pass, specs). [wmTkiF23GRQ, AnL49w4yIOw]

Research shortcuts he uses: a library of real app screens (Mobbin) before any project
[qmJVN2158Mk]; "Frankenstein wireframes" for landing pages, where sites you admire are
screenshotted at 1440 and 375 px, cropped into sections, stacked, locked at low opacity, and
your own version is designed on top, taking structure but never the design itself. [40UAK-LKheA]
